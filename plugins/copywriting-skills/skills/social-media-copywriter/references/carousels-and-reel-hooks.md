# Carousels and Reel Hooks

## Carousel copy structure

A carousel is a multi-slide narrative, not a caption split across images — it needs a beginning, middle, and end, and every slide has to earn the swipe to the next one.

**Shape:**
1. **Cover slide (the hook)** — a bold, specific statement that works completely on its own, since most viewers only ever see this slide. Treat it like a headline: 5-8 words, large type, no hedging.
2. **Middle slides** — build a clear narrative or a sequence of actionable insights. Each slide should advance the idea, not just restate the cover in different words. Curiosity-driving techniques work well mid-carousel:
   - **Open loops** — introduce an idea without fully resolving it, so the reader keeps swiping to find the payoff.
   - **Teasers** — show a glimpse of value without giving everything away on one slide.
   - **Surprising facts** — a real stat or unexpected claim that challenges an assumption.
3. **Final slide (the close)** — a motivational takeaway or an explicit call-to-action. Don't let a carousel just trail off; the last slide should tell the reader exactly what to do next.

**Voice for this format:** carousels tend to reward being **provocative and bold** — challenging conventional wisdom, taking a clear stance rather than hedging, and naming the harder or less-discussed truths in a space rather than repeating generic advice. Pair that boldness with real empathy for the audience's actual struggle; the tone that works best is "tough love," not just contrarianism for its own sake. Keep language simple and direct — every word should earn its place, with no filler. Never fabricate a statistic; if a real number isn't available, don't invent one.

**Carousel first-slide formula** (cover slide, stops the scroll in under a second):
1. Bold headline — 5-8 words max, large font.
2. A visual pattern interrupt — a bold color block, unexpected image, or high-contrast design choice.
3. A directional cue — an arrow, "Swipe →," or another visual pull toward the next slide.
4. Optional subtitle — one line of context in smaller type, only if the headline needs it.

## Reel hooks

Reel hooks are short — usually delivered as on-screen text across the first few seconds of a video — so they need to work as a sequence of short, punchy lines rather than a paragraph. Output them as **Line 1 / Line 2 / Line 3...** so each line maps to a moment on screen. The first line has to stop the scroll on its own; the final line should tease the payoff and prompt an action ("Here's how ↓" or "Drop \[KEYWORD\] for more").

### The six hook formulas

1. **Contrarian/Disruptive** — open by directly contradicting common advice or a widely-held belief in the space. Works because it creates an immediate "wait, what?" reaction.
2. **Shocking Statistic** — lead with a real, specific, surprising number. Precision reads as more credible than a round figure — "73%" lands harder than "most."
3. **Problem-Solution-Twist** — name a familiar problem, hint at the expected solution, then subvert it with an unexpected angle. The twist is what makes it worth watching to the end.
4. **Myth-Reality-Impact** — state a common myth, correct it with the reality, then show what that correction actually means for the viewer's results or decisions.
5. **Before-After-Bridge** — paint the "before" state, the "after" state, then position the content itself as the bridge between them.
6. **Secret-Story-Strategy** — frame the hook as revealing something not commonly shared, deliver it through a short story or moment, then land on the actual strategy or takeaway.

### Viral trigger elements to weave into any hook

- **Controversy** — challenge an accepted norm.
- **Relatability** — create an "I see myself in this" moment.
- **FOMO** — imply urgency or exclusivity.
- **Validation** — confirm a suspicion or frustration the audience already has.
- **Pattern interrupt** — break the expected rhythm or setup the viewer anticipates.
- **Emotional spike** — aim for a specific feeling (joy, frustration, relief, hope), not a neutral tone.

### Non-negotiables

- The first line must stop the scroll — treat it with the same weight as a carousel cover slide.
- Keep language short, punchy, and in active voice; contractions are fine and usually preferred for a casual read-aloud feel.
- Avoid weak qualifiers ("maybe," "might," "could") — hooks work by being definite.
- Never fabricate a statistic; ask for a real one or write around its absence.
- Match the hook formula and brand voice guidelines already established for the account — don't drift into a generic tone just because a formula technically fits.
