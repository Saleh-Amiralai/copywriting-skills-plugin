# Threads and Short-Form Posts

Threads-style platforms (Threads, X) reward three distinct post lengths, each doing a different job. Pick the length based on the goal, not by default.

## 1. Short quote-style one-liners — built for virality

A single sharp statement, often just 1-3 lines, with no story or setup. It works like an aphorism: confident, quotable, and self-contained enough to be reshared without context.

**Shape:** state a counter-intuitive rule or truth directly, then optionally add one line of consequence.

**Example pattern:**
> If a task takes less than 60 minutes and moves things forward — do it now. Everything else? Schedule it or scrap it.

**When to use it:** for a genuinely strong, standalone insight that doesn't need a story to land. If it needs explanation to make sense, it's not a one-liner — move it into the storytelling-arc format instead.

## 2. Mid-length storytelling arcs — built for engagement and connection

Roughly 5-10 short lines telling a compressed personal or client story: a setup, a shift or realization, a concrete outcome, and a closing line that reframes the story as a universal principle.

**Shape:**
1. Set the scene or state the old belief/behavior in one line.
2. Show the tension or struggle plainly — don't rush past the hard part.
3. Name the shift — what changed, and often *why* (a specific trigger, not just "then I got better").
4. State the concrete result, ideally with a real number.
5. Close with a short, quotable line that generalizes the story into a takeaway.

**Example pattern:**
> My first offer was a $49 mini-course. I was terrified no one would buy. My hands shook when I hit post.
> Four people bought that day. $196.
> But those four buyers gave me something bigger: data, feedback, proof people wanted this.
> That $196 became $196K over time, because I kept improving it.
> Never underestimate the power of the first imperfect offer.

**When to use it:** when the point genuinely benefits from a real moment — a specific failure, a specific win, a specific piece of advice that changed something. Vague or generic stories ("I learned so much this year") don't earn the format; specificity is what makes it work.

## 3. Long-form teaching threads — built for authority and saves

10+ lines that actually teach something — a mental model, a system, or a reframe of a common belief — rather than just telling a story. These get saved and referenced back to, which is a stronger long-term signal than a quick like.

**Shape:** open with a clear, slightly provocative claim or reframe, then build the reasoning out step by step (often addressing an objection or misconception along the way), and land on a closing line that ties the whole argument together memorably.

**Example pattern (condensed):**
> AI isn't coming for your job. It's coming for your inefficiency.
> The people who learn to use it well will outpace everyone else.
> But here's the catch: it's only as smart as what you feed it. No context, no frameworks, no examples — it spits back fluff.
> Teach it your voice and your systems, and you're not outsourcing thinking. You're multiplying it.
> The future isn't AI vs. humans. It's humans with AI. And that combination is unstoppable.

**When to use it:** for genuinely substantive ideas worth unpacking — a framework, a shift in how to think about something, a system with more than one moving part. If the idea fits in two lines, it belongs in the one-liner format instead; forcing it long just dilutes it.

## The common thread across all three lengths

Almost every strong post in any of these lengths ends on a short, symmetric, quotable closing line — a compressed restatement of the whole point ("Fast is fun. Deep is profitable." / "Rest isn't a reward — it's strategy." / "Pivoting isn't starting over. It's leveling up."). Write that closing line deliberately, as its own step, rather than letting the post just trail off — it's often the single most-quoted part of the post.

Other techniques that show up repeatedly and are worth reusing: numbered rule-of-3 lists ("3 things I'd do differently"), single-line paragraphs with real line breaks between them (never dense blocks, even in the long-form version), and naming a specific dollar figure, time period, or count rather than a vague quantity whenever a real one is available.
