# LinkedIn

## What the algorithm actually rewards

LinkedIn's feed ranking runs on hundreds of signals, but the pattern that matters most for copywriting is simple: passive signals (a click, a skip, a long dwell time) get read, but active signals (a like, and especially a comment or share) carry real weight — and demographic fields are explicitly not part of ranking. Exceptional posts can travel beyond a poster's own network to reach non-followers interested in the topic, which is the main way LinkedIn reach compounds.

Hashtags do **not** affect feed distribution on LinkedIn, despite persistent advice to the contrary — use them for organization/tracking, not as a reach strategy. What does affect reach: engagement-bait patterns like "Comment YES below" are actively suppressed as a known spam pattern, as are generic recycled "thought leadership" posts and video/text pairings that don't actually relate to each other.

Native formats consistently outperform: documents (PDF/PPT-style carousels) post the highest average engagement rate of any LinkedIn format, followed by multi-image posts. Personal profiles significantly outperform company pages for organic engagement — if there's a choice, post from a person, not a brand page.

## Format specs

- **Standard post**: ≤3,000 characters. Articles (long-form): up to 125,000 characters.
- **Video**: up to 15 minutes, but the meaningful window is the first 5 seconds — that's what determines whether it gets watched at all.
- **Document/carousel posts**: PPT, DOC, or PDF, up to 300 pages, max 100MB.
- **Image posts**: up to 20 images; the first image's orientation sets the layout for the whole post.
- A post can't combine an uploaded image and a URL link preview at the same time — pick one.

## Post structure: Hook, Story, Lesson, CTA

This is the framework almost every high-performing LinkedIn post follows, in practice as well as in theory:

1. **Hook** — one punchy, specific, often counterintuitive opening line. A number, a rejected deal, a confession, a "plot twist" — never a generic intro. This is the only line guaranteed to be read before the "see more" fold (roughly the first 3 lines / ~200 characters), so it has to work standalone.
2. **Credibility beat** — a brief line establishing scale or experience ("after 12 years," "across 40 client accounts") that earns the right to the lesson that follows.
3. **A specific scene or anecdote** — one concrete moment, ideally with a real quoted line of dialogue, that grounds the abstract point in something that actually happened.
4. **The reframe / hard truth** — delivered as a short, standalone-feeling statement, sometimes broken into a 3-item bullet list of principles.
5. **The contrast** — what most people get wrong, positioning this approach as the exception rather than the norm.
6. **A quantified outcome** — a specific number that closes the loop the hook opened.
7. **A universalizing line** — pulls the one specific story up into a general principle the reader can apply.
8. **CTA as a question** — inviting a comment reply, never a hard pitch. "What would you add?" or a simple, easy-to-answer prompt works better than a sales ask.

Formatting that supports this: single-line paragraphs, real line breaks (never dense blocks), bullet points (•) for grouped items, and 3-5 hashtags placed only at the very end, never woven into the copy.

## Hook styles

Surprising statistics or claims, contrarian viewpoints, personal achievements stated with humility, intriguing questions, "I was wrong about..." admissions, unexpected twists ("Plot twist:..."), time-anchored openers ("Just discovered..."), and carefully-balanced polarizing statements. All of these work because they're specific and slightly unexpected — never resort to a flat "Here's what I learned" opener; it reads as generic in a feed full of them.

## Content-type menu

Personal transformation stories, simplified case studies, industry myth-debunking, behind-the-scenes insight, listicles, professionally-framed controversial opinions, data-driven insights, failure-stories-with-a-lesson, quick tactical tips, milestone/celebration posts, curated book or event takeaways, and "what I'm working on now" updates. Rotating through these keeps a posting cadence from feeling repetitive.

## Hashtag strategy (when used for organization, not reach)

A 3-tier approach works well: one primary/broad tag (a large, well-trafficked topic tag), one or two secondary/niche tags specific to the post's actual subject, and optionally one broader trending tag. Cap total hashtags at 3-5 — more reads as spammy without adding reach, since (as above) hashtags don't drive LinkedIn's distribution anyway.

## What suppresses reach

External links pasted directly into the post body noticeably reduce reach — put the link in the first comment instead. Coordinated liking/resharing, comment automation, and engagement pods are actively detected and penalized. Avoid emoji/reaction polls, "double-tap" style engagement-bait, and chain-letter-style engagement requests — all explicitly flagged as spam patterns.

## Cadence

Two to five posts per week is a solid default; posting even twice a week is associated with a meaningfully higher volume of profile views than posting less. Posting more than once a day doesn't appear to hurt reach directly, but spacing out posts on the same topic avoids audience fatigue.
