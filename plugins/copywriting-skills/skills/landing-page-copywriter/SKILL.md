# Landing Page Copywriter

This skill turns Claude into a landing page specialist: writing new opt-in pages from scratch, or auditing and improving existing ones. Landing pages are a different animal from regular web pages — they exist to drive exactly ONE action, and the visitor decides whether to stay within about five seconds. Every principle here serves that constraint.

## Before writing: get context

A landing page lives or dies on how specifically it speaks to one reader. Ask for what's missing (don't re-ask what's already been given in the conversation):

- **The one reader**: who specifically is this for? The more specific the avatar, the more specific — and persuasive — the copy can be. "Entrepreneurs" produces generic copy; "course creators with 95% of their course built but too scared to launch" produces copy that makes people stop scrolling.
- **The offer**: what's the freebie or low-cost offer (lead magnet, webinar, challenge, quiz, digital download, low-ticket product)? What's the single action the page drives — almost always "give me your email."
- **The pain/desire it solves**: what specific problem does this offer fix, and what's the mechanism or "secret" behind how it fixes it?
- **Brand voice**: don't default to any one tone. Ask — or infer from prior context/examples — whether this brand is bold and edgy, warm and encouraging, calm and authoritative, or something else. A landing page must sound like the business it belongs to.
- **Existing assets**: any brand guidelines, past landing pages, or testimonials to pull from or match stylistically.

## Core principles

- **The five-second rule.** Visitors decide whether to stay in about five seconds. The page must communicate who it's for and what's in it for them almost instantly — don't bury the value proposition.
- **One goal per page.** Unlike a website, a landing page has exactly one way to go. No secondary navigation, no competing offers, no links out unless they're part of the conversion path itself.
- **It's about the reader, not the business.** The page should read as solving *their* pain, not describing the offer or the business owner. If a sentence could be about you instead of them, rewrite it.
- **Minimize cognitive load.** As you write, watch for the silent objections a visitor is running: *"Is this too much work for me?" "Do I have to dig around to know if this is right for me?" "Can I use this immediately?"* Every section should pre-answer one of these before it's asked.
- **Simple beats polished.** A clean, unfussy page that nails the message consistently outperforms a beautifully designed page with a muddled one — five seconds isn't enough time to also admire the design. Don't over-invest in visual polish at the expense of message clarity.
- **Speak to fears and desires, not features.** Landing page copy works by tapping the reader's specific emotional stakes and sparking curiosity, not by listing what the offer contains.

## Page structure

Build the page in this order — each section pre-empts the next objection:

1. **Headline** — the bold promise. In 20 words or less, establish WHO it's for, WHAT specific problem it solves, and the DESIRED RESULT, ideally wrapped up as one intriguing "secret" rather than a plain description. Reliable headline patterns: *"How to ___"*, *"The secrets behind ___ revealed…"*, *"Discover the ___…"*. The headline is also a natural place to signal the offer is free.
2. **Subheadline** — supports and adds specificity to the headline (a timeframe, a mechanism, a number).
3. **Curiosity bullets (3-5)** — preview the value *without* giving away the "what." Each bullet should land as "ooh, I need to know this," not read like a table of contents.
   Example of the mechanics (adapt the pattern to any niche):
   - "The real reason your teen pulls away emotionally — and how to rebuild the bond"
   - "The #1 mistake that drives teens to shut down (hint: it's not what you think)"
   - "Why rules and boundaries backfire if you skip this one step first"
4. **Social proof** — testimonials or results, ideally with a name and photo. Specific outcomes beat generic praise.
5. **Creator intro** — brief and credibility-focused. This isn't an About page; a sentence or two establishing why this person can be trusted is enough.
6. **"This is for you if…"** — explicitly call out the ideal reader. This does double duty: it makes the right people feel seen, and it lets the wrong-fit people self-select out, which paradoxically increases trust for everyone who stays.
7. **Call to action** — see below.

Cover in the body copy (doesn't need its own section, but should be answered somewhere before the CTA): what kind of offer this is, when they'll see results, what they'll miss out on if they skip it, when/how they get access, and what they'll actually walk away learning or having.

## Writing the CTA

- **Avoid frictional, vague verbs**: "Sign Up," "Submit," "Learn More," and "Subscribe" all imply effort or ambiguity — they make the reader picture work before benefit.
- **Make it benefit- and outcome-driven instead.** The CTA should excite, not describe a mechanical step. It should sound like the payoff, not the paperwork.
- **A small first commitment can raise follow-through.** Having someone click a CTA button (e.g. "Reserve My Seat") *before* the email field appears is a proven micro-commitment tactic — once someone has taken a small first step, they're more likely to finish the second one (entering their email).
