# Ad Copy Frameworks — Full Detail and Examples

Full implementation steps for each framework from SKILL.md, plus real patterns worth borrowing (paraphrased and genericized from real-world ads, not tied to any one brand).

## Hook-Story-Offer

**Why it works:** mirrors the natural persuasion path — grab attention, build emotional connection, present a value proposition that makes "yes" feel obvious. The story segment specifically lowers skepticism, because people drop their guard during narrative in a way they don't during a pitch.

**How to implement:**
1. **Hook** — one line: "I tried 5 side hustles — only one made me $10K/month (here's why)…"
2. **Story** — 3-5 sentences, outcome-focused: the struggle, the turning point, the shift. ("I went from 14-hour days to retiring my 9-5, after stumbling on a strategy…")
3. **Offer** — the product or lead magnet as the vehicle for that same outcome, with what makes it valuable (bonuses, speed, guarantee), closed with a direct CTA.

**Adaptation:** works in any coaching niche. A fitness coach might hook with a near-miss moment, tell the story of finding a safer method, then offer the program. A leadership consultant might hook with a startling industry stat, tell a CEO transformation story, then invite a strategy call. The offer has to feel genuinely high-value relative to its cost — that's what makes the "yes" easy.

## Problem-Agitate-Solution (PAS)

**Why it works:** leverages problem-awareness and relief. Naming the problem first (rather than leading with the solution) hooks readers who feel seen; agitating it (empathetically, not with shame) increases urgency to act; the solution then reads as genuine relief rather than a hard sell.

**How to implement:** open bluntly or with a pointed question ("Struggling to get prospects to show up to your webinars?"). Empathize and agitate — describe the felt cost of the problem in the audience's own emotional language ("It's frustrating pouring hours into a webinar only to have single-digit attendees..."). Pivot to the solution as the way out, close with a CTA.

**Guardrail:** keep the agitation empathetic, not accusatory — "we understand how exhausting this is," not "you are failing." In B2B, agitation often centers on lost revenue or falling behind competitors; in B2C, it's more often emotional (frustration, embarrassment, exhaustion).

## Before-After-Bridge

**Why it works:** sells the transformation directly by exploiting contrast — the pain of the current state versus the appeal of the future state — with the offer positioned as the one thing that closes the gap.

**How to implement:** describe a specific, relatable "before" ("Right now, you spend your evenings cold-pitching on LinkedIn with zero replies…"), then a vivid "after" with real emotional detail ("Imagine waking up to 5 eager inquiries each morning, without sending a single cold message…"), then introduce the offer explicitly as the bridge between the two, closing with a CTA to take the first step.

**Adaptation:** scale the drama of the contrast to the real stakes of the niche. A life coach addressing mild dissatisfaction should use a subtler contrast ("feeling a bit lost" → "waking up with clarity") than a business offer promising a dramatic turnaround. Overhyping the gap breaks trust — the transformation has to stay believable.

## Power Offer (Joel Erway's 2-sentence ad)

**Why it works:** targets only the most ready 5-10% of the market — people who already know they have the problem and want a fast solution — by skipping the usual nurture entirely and leading with a blunt, specific value proposition. Zero ambiguity appeals to decisive buyers.

**Formula:** "If I offered to \[deliver a specific result\] without \[the usual pain or objection\], would you \[take the desired action\]?"
Example: "If I offered to 10x your e-commerce ROAS in 90 days without increasing your ad budget, would you take me up on it?"

**Guidelines:** the promised result must be genuinely compelling — a no-brainer, not a modest improvement — and ideally something that can be backed up with proof once the reader clicks through. Naming a common objection in the same breath ("without X") is what makes it land as more than a bold claim.

**Adaptation:** works best for outcome-driven offers, especially B2B or high-ticket B2C, where the result is easy to state in one line. Less natural for nuanced transformations ("find inner peace" doesn't compress well) — but it can still be adapted: "If I offered you a step-by-step plan to overcome anxiety in 30 days without medication, would you want to see it?" Good for a quick-win test to see whether the core offer really entices at first glance.

## Tried-and-Failed Market

**Why it works:** targets prospects who've already attempted to solve the problem unsuccessfully — often the most qualified leads, because they're actively searching, skeptical, and want something that actually works. Naming what they've tried and failed at positions the new offer as the smarter, more informed option.

**How to implement:**
- Define the audience by past actions, not demographics: "If you've tried X and Y, but still struggle with Z…"
- Name the specific flawed strategy they've likely tried.
- Introduce a new mechanism early — this is the differentiator.
- Frame it as finally explaining *why* nothing else worked.

**Structure:** Hook (name the failed method) → Agitate (the fatigue of trying and failing) → Reveal (the unique mechanism) → Offer (the bridge to the product).

**Adaptation:** especially effective for jaded or "burned" buyers — coaching clients who've tried multiple programs, marketers fatigued by webinar funnels. The hook and the "new mechanism" both need to feel genuinely distinct — rehashing the same promises the audience has already heard undermines the entire angle.

## Us/Them (New Way)

**Why it works:** creates identity-based contrast — the offer is the "New Way," everything else is the outdated "Old Way." This taps belonging as much as logic ("people like us do things like this").

**Structure:** Avatar + pain → Big promise → Authority → Old Way vs. New Way breakdown → Explanation of the new model → CTA.

**Adaptation:** works well in saturated niches with an obvious default method to critique — course creators, service providers, or any space where there's a clear conventional approach to position against. The contrast needs a real, nameable "old way" to work; it falls flat in niches without an obvious default.

## Why Me

**Why it works:** built for warm audiences actively comparing options — people who are already asking "why should I trust *this* person with this problem?" It answers that question logically while reinforcing emotional connection, functioning as case-maker copy rather than awareness-building.

**Structure:** Attention + avatar → Offer promise → Relevance/context → Why this matters now → Why you (credentials, unique POV, personal story) → Proof → Offer + CTA.

**Adaptation:** especially strong for middle-of-funnel retargeting, webinar invites, or warming up an email list that's already engaged. Not the right tool for genuinely cold, top-of-funnel awareness — it assumes the reader already knows the category and is evaluating who to trust within it.
